# How to edit Wikipedia pages

Editing Wikipedia pages is pretty straightforward and there are lots of resources available on the topic! Here’s a summary nonetheless. 

## Steps

1. [Make an account.](https://auth.wikimedia.org/enwiki/wiki/Special:CreateAccount?useformat=desktop&usesul3=1&centralauthLoginToken=e69560aef1f1fe8457fdb6825e13d7f1)
2. Click ‘Edit’ at the top of any Wikipedia page.
3. You can use the visual editor (simpler) or the source editor (writing a language called ‘[wiki markup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Wiki_markup)’ directly—slightly more technical). 

Here is the [more detailed Wikipedia guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Introduction_to_editing_with_Wiki_Markup/1) on editing with Wiki Markup. 

## Content and principles

Here is a brief summary of some of Wikipedia’s core content policies; much of this is taken from its ‘[Everything you need to know](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Everything_you_need_to_know)’ section.  

1. [**No original research**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research) - in other words, everything has to be cited from a reliable source. What can be considered ‘reliable’ can be debated, of course, but generally this might include:
    - reputable news outlets
    - respected publishers, eg an academic publisher
    
    ‘Original research’ refers to your own analysis, or information you have come across independently—not stated by the reputable sources directly. 
    
    Note that plagiarism is also firmly not allowed—ensure all information can be cited, but do not copy from those sources directly. 
    
2. [**Neutral point of view**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view) - while objectivity can, of course, be problematised (see ‘situated knowledges’ in ["Why not" section below](#why-not-or-problematics)), Wikipedia content should be written in a way that ‘represent[s] fairly, proportionately, and, as far as possible, without editorial bias’.
3. [**Verifiability**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability) - facts and claims should be able to be checked and confirmed via those reliable sources mentioned above. ‘If reliable sources disagree with each other, then maintain a [neutral point of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view) and present what the various sources say, giving each side its [due weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view#Undue_weight).’ Anything that can be disputed should have an inline citation to a source that confirms the claim. 
    
    [For more on citations, see this page.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources)
    
4. [**No LLM-generated content**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Writing_articles_with_large_language_models) - while generated text is not allowed, AI proofreading and editing *is—*so long as you, the human editor, review all changes. AI-generated content may be deleted and editors that frequently contravene this rule may be banned. 

# Why?

## Why edit Wikipedia in the first place?

For me, there are a few core reasons as to what editing Wikipedia affords:

- It’s a highly effective way of disseminating research and contributing to the public domain.
    
    While I don’t think reach and scale are the most important things when it comes to research—for example, an in-depth conversation with an interlocutor might be just as valuable—but it’s a pretty unique and exciting affordance. Arguably, my research would not have as much of an audience anywhere else! 
    
- Wikipedia is a [common source of LLM training data](https://medium.com/wikiblueprint/ai-and-llms-have-changed-wikipedias-importance-forever-8ef85d847bf0), and frequently shapes search engine results too. (This source I’ve linked would not qualify as a reputable one for a Wikipedia page!)
    
    Here’s a half-baked, abstract prompt: what can be seeded by gesturing towards more entangled epistemologies in LLM training data? Maybe an affordance of AI is in its ability to help cultivate “hold[ing] multiple, even conflicting, truths without rushing to collapse them,” or in other words, to hold complexity. “[Everything Is Nature: Meta-Relationality, Nervous Systems, Systems Thinking, and AI](https://files.metarelationality.institute/everything-is-nature.pdf)”, along with much of the other work of Vanessa Machao de Oliveira and the [Meta-Relationality Institute](https://metarelationality.institute/), inquires along these lines.
    

## Why edit *plant* Wikipedia pages specifically?

The points above are relevant, of course—dissemination via Wikipedia might be an important way for us to share less common knowledges, folk knowledges, and critical perspectives (eg on plant naming conventions and taxonomy, on specific uses for specific plants). 

- **Disseminating folk knowledges and critical perspectives** - for instance on plant naming conventions and taxonomy, or specific herbalist applications of specific plants. I imagine that much of [Banu Subramaniam’s *Botany of Empire*](https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295752464/botany-of-empire/) (published by a reputable press!) could offer really important perspectives largely missing from much current plant Wiki literature.
    - There’s a counter-perspective here around the assimilation of folk, Indigenous and other knowledges into a monoculture—[see below](#why-not-or-problematics) for more on this.
- **Supporting offline use** - downloading all of Wikipedia is a practice that people engage in for “[doomsday preparedness](https://www.reddit.com/r/DataHoarder/comments/1fccptq/did_you_know_you_can_download_all_of_wikipedia_in/)”. Practical herbalism could be invaluable here.
- **Contributing to digital literacy and agency** - in creating this guide, it’s a small intention to help cultivate these skills, particularly in a sphere that is often holds tech at arms’ length.*
- **Countering the bias** - English Wikipedia’s editorship and subjects are highly skewed:
    
    > Pokémon was more comprehensively covered than places in sub-Saharan Africa. The Wikimedia Foundation, using data from the past few years, maintains a page on different forms of underrepresentation: Antarctica has more articles than many countries in Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Less than 15 percent of Wikipedia editors are women, and in the United States less than 1 percent are black. Of biographies of people on English Wikipedia, about 80 percent are men.
    (via [Megan Marz “Cabinet of Curiosities” in *The Baffler*](https://thebaffler.com/latest/cabinet-of-curiosities-marz))
    > 
- **It’s fun** 🤓 - and a nice way of deepening our (potential editors’) plant knowledges too.

* It’s maybe worth stating that I am firmly not a tech solutionist! No technology is neutral, but we do use digital technologies every day, and I think it’s worth thinking about each technology’s affordances and engaging in them more deeply. 

## Why *not*? (Or ‘problematics’)

- **You do not want to feed a monoculture** - *the flipside of inclusion is assimilation*. *Should* something be included here? What are ways in which it could be weaponised or utilised? Does it make anyone, anything vulnerable in some way? What are the risks of visibility?
    
    These are very valid and worthwhile questions! I also like to remember how at the edges of ecosystems, or where different ecosystems (including monocultures) meet, there tends to be more diversity.
    
- **You are critical of LLMs and want nothing to do with them** - and arguably the way they currently use Wikipedia is extractive, and does the platform a disservice.
    
    > LLMs heavily use Wikipedia as training data and repay the favor by keeping users for themselves, delivering answers directly in the chat or on the results page…These are the problems of the entire open web, which platforms and *especially LLMs continuously digest and excrete in less nutritious formats*.
    (emphasis mine, via [Megan Marz “Cabinet of Curiosities” in *The Baffler*](https://thebaffler.com/latest/cabinet-of-curiosities-marz))
 
    
    I won’t get into energy usage, water usage, data centres, resource extraction or any of the rest of that here. But I’ll offer a counterpoint here too, via Vanessa Machado de Oliveira:
    
    > The refusal to engage with AI was itself a form of…phantom agency: the performance of action in conditions where the steering has already been disconnected. Writing critiques of AI on platforms whose logic the critiques oppose…Refusing to touch the tool as though the tool were not already inside your lungs, your data, your students’ attention spans, *the very infrastructure through which your refusal circulates*.
    > (emphasis mine, via [Vanessa Machado de Oliveira and Peter M. Senge, “Everything is Nature”](https://files.metarelationality.institute/everything-is-nature.pdf))
    
- **You do not believe that ‘neutral’ knowledge is desirable nor possible** (aka the ‘situated knowledges’ point) - this is a key perspective in [feminist science and technology studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist_science_and_technology_studies); [Donna Haraway’s “Situated Knowledges”](https://michaeltbacon.github.io/files/Haraway%20-%201988%20-%20Situated%20Knowledges%20The%20Science%20Question%20in%20Femin.pdf) posited that the closest we can get to ‘objective’ knowledge is to acknowledge the way any knowledge or framework is *situated*. [Vandana Shiva, in “Monocultures of the Mind”](http://trumpeter.athabascau.ca/index.php/trumpet/article/view/358/563) wrote that the supposedly universal, Western knowledge system is, in fact, a *local* system that has scaled to *appear* universal.

Ultimately I don’t think anything is one-size-fits-all, that there is one ‘right’ way of engaging with anything. To me, learning to dance with these knotty, specific affordances and problematics is a key part of critical research.
